FACTBOX

Privacy Policy

Effective 2 September 2026 Applies to: factbox.app, this website

Factbox is a website with real accounts, and a small amount of server behind them. Almost everything it remembers about you is written into your own browser and stays there. This page lists what it writes, the few things that do leave your browser, and how to get rid of any of it.

Not reviewed by a lawyer. This is written to describe what the site genuinely does today, checked line by line against the code that does it. It has not been through legal review.

The short version

  • You can sign in with Google, an email address and password, or a phone number. Your account is held by Google Firebase; we never see your password. Signing in is what makes a subscription follow you to another phone.
  • If you are signed in, your onboarding answers are saved to your own record. If you are not, nothing is copied anywhere and they stay in this browser.
  • That email leaves your browser in exactly one place: it is put into the email field of the Stripe checkout page, so you do not have to type it twice.
  • What you have read, how far into each story, and which stories you saved are written to your browser's storage and are never sent anywhere.
  • Three small values — whether you have access, your restore token, and where that access came from — are also mirrored into a first-party cookie, because in-app browsers wipe the other store.
  • Payment is handled entirely by Stripe, on Stripe's own pages. Card details never touch this site.
  • Your restore link is a bearer token: anyone holding it has access.
  • Every page loads PostHog to measure which stories people finish, including how long each card is on screen. It stores a random id in your browser. There is a one-tap off switch in section 04.
  • Clearing this site's data in your browser deletes everything the site holds. You do not have to ask us, because there is no copy anywhere else.

02 · What the site stores in your browser

These are the exact keys, as written by the code. Nothing on this list is transmitted to us or to anyone else; the site has no endpoint to send them to.

Every localStorage key this site writes
KeyWhat it holds
fb_unlocked_v1Whether this browser has access. The value is the single character 1, or nothing at all.
fb_pass_v1Your restore token, so the restore link can be rebuilt on demand. See §05.
fb_passsrc_v1Where access came from: stripe, restore or local.
fb_read_v1Your reading memory, as one small JSON object: per story, the furthest card you reached, how many cards that story had, a whole-second timestamp of when you last touched it, and a finished flag. Capped at 60 stories and about 20 KB; the oldest entries are dropped first.
fb_saved_v1Stories you saved, as a list of story id and a whole-second timestamp, newest first. Capped at 200 entries and about 8 KB.
fb_saveprobeWritten and immediately deleted once per page load, purely to find out whether this browser will accept a write at all.
fb_owner_v1Set only if someone unlocks the site with the owner passphrase. It exists so the person who makes the site can read it on a new phone without paying.
fb_acct_v1The sign-up record, as one small JSON object: a randomly generated local id, the email address you typed, your first name if you gave one, the topics you picked, how often you said you wanted to read, which plan you chose, whether you finished the onboarding questions, and when the record was made. Capped at 700 bytes; if it ever hit the cap, the topic picks are dropped before the email is.
fb-soundWhether you switched a story’s ambient sound on or off, so it does not have to ask again.
fb-sound-hintSet once, after you have been shown the note about your phone's silent switch, so it is never shown twice.

One further value is kept in sessionStorage, which your browser discards when you close the tab: fb-story, the id of the illustrated story the front page is currently showing.

Story ids are strings like 07B. With the single exception of fb_acct_v1, no key on this list contains a name, an email address, an identifier for you, or anything that would tell us who you are if we could read it — which we cannot.

The sign-up record

Because there is no server, "sign up" and "log in" cannot mean what they usually mean. There is nobody to register with and nothing to authenticate against, which was why the form once had no password field: at the time a password implied a check we could not to perform.

What signing up actually does is write fb_acct_v1 above into your own browser. Your email and name sit there and nowhere else. We never receive them, and logging in on a second phone cannot move your access, because there is nothing on our side that knows you — the restore link in §05 is the only bridge that exists, and the sign-up page says so in those words rather than implying magic.

The onboarding questions — which topics interest you, how often you want to read — are answered into the same local record. As things stand nothing outside the sign-up page reads them. Every one of them can be skipped, and a skip is stored as a skip rather than filled in with a guess.

03 · The cookie

The site sets first-party cookies under four names: fb_unlocked_v1, fb_pass_v1 and fb_passsrc_v1 — the three access values above — and fb_acct_v1, the sign-up record, which is mirrored only while it stays under 700 bytes and is silently skipped if it grows past that. They are set for one year on path=/ with SameSite=Lax, and with the Secure flag when the page is served over HTTPS. There are no others.

The reason is specific. Most readers arrive from Instagram or TikTok, and those apps open links in an in-app browser that routinely throws away localStorage between sessions while keeping cookies. Without the cookie, a person who paid inside Instagram would be asked to pay again the next day. The site reads whichever of the two stores still has the value, and repairs the other.

Your reading memory and your saved stories are never put in a cookie. They are far too large, and a cookie rides along on every request — which would mean sending your reading history to the web server on every page load. They stay in localStorage, which is sent nowhere.

These cookies are strictly functional: they remember a purchase you made. They do not identify you, follow you to other sites, or feed any advertising system.

04 · Analytics

Every page loads PostHog, a product-analytics service, hosted in the United States. It is how we find out which stories people finish and which ones lose them, which is the only way the next ones get better.

PostHog stores an identifier in your browser so that two visits from the same browser can be recognised as the same reader. That is not the cookieless arrangement an earlier version of this page described, and it is the part worth knowing: it is what makes "did anyone come back for a second story" answerable. It is a random id, tied to no name, and it is not shared with anyone. Turning it off is one tap, below.

This is the complete list of event names the site sends:

  • library_view, explore_view, library_own_view — a section of the site was opened.
  • stack_open, resume_used, stack_complete, stack_dropoff — a story was opened, resumed, finished, or left part-way, carrying the story id and the card number reached.
  • save_add, save_remove, library_unsave, rec_save — a story was saved or unsaved, carrying the story id.
  • rec_view, rec_click — end-of-story recommendations were shown or followed, carrying the story id and which slot was tapped.
  • paywall_view, subscribe_click, join_view, join_step, join_skip, join_interests, checkout_start — the subscribe screen or a step of the sign-up flow was shown, skipped or completed, carrying the step name, the number of topics picked, or the plan chosen. Not the answers themselves, and never your email address.
  • join_signup, join_login_hit, join_login_known, join_login_miss, join_restore_use — someone signed up, tried to log in, or used a restore link. These carry no properties at all.
  • owner_unlock — the site was unlocked with the owner's own passphrase.
  • card_view — a card left the screen, carrying the story id, the card number, its narrative beat, its subject, and how many seconds it was on screen. This is the one that tells us where a story loses people. Anything under a second is treated as a swipe passing through and is not sent.

Every one of those carries at most a story id, a card number, a step name or a plan name. None of them carries your email address, your name, or the content of your answers.

The illustrated front-page story contains a second, older counter of its own. Its endpoint is an empty string, so it writes its line to the browser's developer console and sends nothing over the network. It is inert.

We also send the same events to Google Analytics. Alongside PostHog, every event listed above goes to Google Analytics 4, through Firebase. It is exactly the same list — the same story ids, card numbers, step names and dwell times — and still never your email address, your name, or the content of your answers. Google stores an identifier in your browser too, for the same reason PostHog does.

Running two analytics services is a duplication, and it is temporary. We are comparing them on the same traffic in order to keep one. Until we do, your visit is measured twice. The switch below turns off both.

Turning it off

Press the button. It takes effect immediately in this browser, is remembered, and stops the analytics script from loading at all on your next visit — not merely from reporting.

A content blocker that blocks posthog.com does the same thing, and the site behaves identically without it — every call is wrapped so that a missing analytics script is simply nothing happening.

05 · Payment, and the restore link

Subscriptions are taken through Stripe, on a checkout page that Stripe hosts. You leave this site to pay and come back when you are done. Your card number, your billing address and your name are typed into Stripe's page, not ours. They never touch this site and we never see them. Stripe is responsible for what you enter there, under its own privacy policy. What comes back to this site is a return address carrying a success flag and a checkout session id, and nothing else.

Two things are added to that checkout address when you go to it, and they are the only things about you that this site ever transmits. The first is your email address as Stripe's prefilled_email, so the payment page's email field is already filled in and you do not type it twice; you can change it there. The second is the random local id from your sign-up record as Stripe's client_reference_id, an arbitrary string that comes back on Stripe's side of the payment so that a payment could later be matched to the browser that made it. Neither is sent anywhere else, and nothing else about you goes with them.

That session id becomes your restore token, stored as fb_pass_v1, and your restore link is that token on the end of a factbox.app address. It is the only way to move access from the browser you paid in to another phone or another browser. Signing in does exactly that, and is now the route we would rather you used; the restore link remains for anyone who subscribed before accounts existed.

The restore link is a bearer token. There is no server to check it against, so possession is the entire proof. Anyone you forward it to gets access, and so does anyone who reads it over your shoulder or finds it in a screenshot. Treat it like a key, not like a receipt.

It contains no personal information: it is a random-looking string plus a four-character check that tells a mistyped link apart from a real one.

06 · What never leaves your browser

Your reading progress. Which stories you opened, how far into each you got, which you finished, which you saved, and when. All of it is written to your own browser and to nothing else. We do not have it, cannot request it, and could not produce it if we were asked to, because there is no server holding it and no endpoint the page could send it to.

The trade is exact and worth stating: nothing about your reading can leak from us, and equally nothing about your reading can be restored by us. Clear your site data and it is gone for good.

07 · Hosting, and what a web server sees

The site is static files hosted on GitHub Pages. Like every web server on earth, it receives the ordinary request information needed to send you a page: your IP address, the address you asked for, your browser's user agent, and the time. That handling is GitHub's, under its own privacy policy. We do not receive or keep those logs.

Every image on the site is served from this domain. Artwork is downloaded from Wikimedia Commons once, in advance, and re-hosted here, so reading a story sends no request to Wikimedia and tells them nothing about you. Following a source or licence link from the credits page is an ordinary outbound link, and that site then sees you the way any site you visit does.

09 · Your rights, and how to use them

Depending on where you live — the UK and EU under the GDPR, California under the CCPA, and many places besides — you have rights to see, correct, export or delete the personal information a company holds about you. Here those rights are unusually easy to exercise, because in the ordinary course we hold nothing.

  • To see or export it: it is in your browser, under the keys listed in §02. Your browser's developer tools will show you every one of them, in full.
  • To delete it: clear this site's data in your browser. On an iPhone that is Settings → Safari → Advanced → Website Data; Chrome and Firefox have the equivalent. That removes every key in §02 and the cookies in §03 in one action, and nothing survives it, on our side or yours.
  • Note that this deletes your access too, because access is one of those keys. If you want to keep it, save your restore link somewhere first.
  • Your email address and your onboarding answers are in fb_acct_v1, in your browser. Clearing site data deletes them, and we hold no copy of either.
  • If you have emailed us: that email is the one thing we actually hold. Ask and we will delete it and confirm that we have.
  • If you have paid: Stripe holds a billing record, and has to, for tax and accounting reasons. A request about that data can come to us and we will pass it on, or you can go to Stripe directly.

We do not sell personal information and never have. There is nothing to sell.

10 · Security

The strongest thing we can say about security is that there is very little surface: no account to break into, no reader database to leak, and no credentials to steal. What is on your device is protected by your device.

Two limits are worth being plain about, because the alternative is you finding out later. The restore link is a bearer token, as §05 says. And the access flag is a value in your own browser rather than a check against a server, so it is something the site remembers rather than something the site enforces; the story text itself is served as a public data file, and a subscription buys the reading experience and supports the work rather than unlocking a vault. That is inherent to a site with no server, and it is the trade we have made for now.

Email is not encrypted end to end. Please do not put anything sensitive in a message to us.
