FACTBOX

Privacy Policy

Effective 3 September 2026 Applies to: factbox.app

Factbox is a subscription you read on the web, and it now has accounts and a server behind them. Most of what it remembers about you is still written into your own browser, where clearing site data is the whole of deleting it. Some of it is not: an account, the answers you gave when you started, what you have read, and a payment all live somewhere your phone cannot reach. This page keeps those two piles apart — what is written where, what leaves the browser, which companies receive it, and how to be rid of each.

The short version

  • You can sign in with Google, or with an email address and a password. The account is held by Google Firebase; your password never reaches us.
  • Signing in creates a record on our side under a Firebase user id. Whether you are a subscriber is written there by Stripe's webhook, and that is what makes a subscription work on a second phone.
  • The six questions at the start of signing up are answered by tapping one of a fixed set of buttons, never by typing, and all six are written into this browser. If you are signed in, two of the six are also copied to your account record — the subjects you picked and the minutes a day you chose — along with your name and email address if you gave them. The other four stay on this device. Section 07 lists that record field by field.
  • What you have read, how far into each story, and which stories you saved are written into this browser. If you are signed in they are also written to your account, which is what carries your place in a story to a second phone. Signed out, they never leave the browser. Section 08 is the whole of it.
  • Payment is handled by Stripe, on Stripe's own pages. Card details never touch our pages.
  • Your restore link is a bearer token: anyone holding it has access.
  • Every page sends events to PostHog and to Google Analytics. Once you are signed in, those events carry your Firebase user id, so they are not anonymous. There is a one-tap off switch in section 05.
  • Every page also loads a font from Google, which means Google sees your IP address on every page whether or not you have an account.
  • Anything you write in the two boxes on the support page is sent to us and stored, along with the reply address you give.
  • Clearing this site's data in your browser removes everything in section 02 and section 03. It does not remove your account, the record under it, or a message you have sent us — for that, see section 11.

02 · What the site stores in your browser

These are the exact keys, as written by the code. Nothing on this list is read off your device by anything other than the site's own code running in it, and while you are signed out none of it leaves at all. Signed in, three of these keys are mirrored to your account and say so: fb_acct_v1 (section 07), and fb_read_v1 and fb_saved_v1 (section 08). Your email address is also separately re-entered at Stripe (section 06).

Four further values are kept in sessionStorage, which your browser discards when you close the tab. fbx_corrected_v1 is set after the page has reloaded itself once to correct a paywall it drew before your subscription had loaded, so it can never do that twice. fbpg_pulled_v1 does the same job for a shelf drawn before your account's reading had arrived. fb_auth_redirect_v1 is set while you are away at Google's sign-in page, so the site knows to expect you back. fb_live_v1 holds the Firebase user id this tab has actually seen signed in, so a page cannot draw personal state on the strength of a stale hint.

Story ids are strings like 07B. With the single exception of fb_acct_v1, no key on this list contains a name, an email address, or anything else that identifies you.

PostHog, Google Analytics and Firebase each keep storage of their own in your browser, under names they choose rather than ones written here. Sections 05 and 09 say what those are for. Clearing this site's data removes them along with everything above.

03 · Cookies

The site sets first-party cookies under four names: fb_unlocked_v1, fb_pass_v1 and fb_passsrc_v1 — the three access values above — and fb_acct_v1, which is mirrored only while it stays under 700 bytes and is silently skipped if it grows past that. They are set for one year on path=/ with SameSite=Lax, and with the Secure flag when the page is served over HTTPS.

The reason is specific. Most readers arrive from Instagram or TikTok, and those apps open links in an in-app browser that routinely throws away localStorage between sessions while keeping cookies. Without the cookie, a person who paid inside Instagram would be asked to pay again the next day. The site reads whichever of the two stores still has the value, and repairs the other.

Your reading memory and your saved stories are never put in a cookie. They are far too large, and a cookie rides along on every request — which would mean sending your reading history to the web server on every page load. They stay in localStorage. Signed out, that is the end of them; signed in, they are written to your account by a request that goes to Firestore and nowhere else, which is section 08.

Those four are the ones our own code sets. They are functional: they remember a purchase you made and what you typed into the funnel. They do not follow you to other sites and they feed no advertising system. The analytics services in section 05 set cookies of their own, on this domain, which is what an identifier for a returning reader is.

04 · Signing in

There are two ways in: Google, and an email address with a password. Both are handled by Google Firebase Authentication. We never see your password — Firebase holds it, and the site has no field, no code and no storage that could keep one. Signing up with an email address makes Firebase send you a verification message. Asking for a password reset makes Firebase send you another. Both leave Google's servers, not ours.

Signing in with Google hands Google the fact that you are signing in to Factbox, and hands us the email address, display name and profile photo on that Google account.

Phone sign-in was offered on an earlier version of these pages. It is gone: there is no phone field, no SMS code and no reCAPTCHA on the site.

05 · Analytics

Every page sends events to PostHog, a product-analytics service hosted in the United States, and to Google Analytics 4 through Firebase. Both get the same events. It is how we find out which stories people finish and which ones lose them.

Running two is a duplication, and it is meant to be temporary: they are being compared on the same traffic so that one can be dropped. Until then your visit is measured twice. The switch below turns off both.

Analytics is only transmitted from factbox.app and www.factbox.app. On any other host the code loads, runs and sends nothing, so a test build cannot report itself as a reader.

What is attached to you

Once you sign in, the events are no longer anonymous. The site calls PostHog's identify with your Firebase user id, which ties this browser to your account and joins the reading you did before you signed in to the same person. From that point every event also carries three properties: whether you have an account, whether you are a subscriber, and which of admin, subscriber, legacy or none your access is. Signed out, no user id is sent and those three still are.

Both services also keep an identifier of their own in your browser, so two visits from the same browser are recognised as the same reader. That is true whether or not you have an account.

Where the requests go

PostHog is not reached at posthog.com. The site sends its analytics to factbox.app/ink/…, a path on this domain served by a Cloudflare Worker that forwards it to PostHog. This is deliberate, and the reason is worth stating plainly: blocker lists match PostHog by hostname, and a request to factbox.app matches no list. A content blocker that blocks PostHog will not stop these events. The off switch below will, and blocking factbox.app/ink/ will.

Two things the Worker does are worth naming. It strips the cookie header before forwarding, so the site's own cookies — including Firebase's — never reach PostHog. And it passes your IP address on in an X-Forwarded-For header, so PostHog places you in a country rather than in a Cloudflare datacentre. If the Worker does not answer, the site falls back to PostHog's own hosts and the events go there directly.

Google Analytics is not proxied. Those events go to Google.

What the events say

This is the complete list of event names, grouped. Every one of them also carries the three properties above, and your user id if you are signed in.

  • page_open — which page was opened, as a name like home or library. The query string is dropped.
  • ui_click — a button or link was tapped, carrying the page and the control's name. The name comes from the element's id or the words printed on it, which are copy written in our own repository. It never reads a text box, a menu or anything you typed.
  • stack_open, resume_used, stack_complete, stack_dropoff, story_time — a story was opened, resumed, finished or left part-way, carrying the story id, the card reached, and how many seconds you were actually reading with the tab in front of you.
  • card_view — a card left the screen, carrying the story id, the card number, its narrative beat, its subject, and how many seconds it was on screen. Anything under a second is treated as a swipe passing through and is not sent.
  • save_add, save_remove, library_unsave, library_view, library_own_view, home_view, rec_view, rec_click — a story was saved or unsaved, a shelf was opened, or an end-of-story recommendation was shown or followed. These carry a story id and which slot was tapped.
  • join_view, join_step, join_skip, join_draw, join_relate, join_time, join_streak, join_praise, join_plan_start, join_plan_ask, join_plan_answer, join_plan_built, join_plan_pick, join_signup, join_login_hit, join_login_known, join_login_miss, join_restore_use — a step of the sign-up funnel was shown, answered, skipped or completed, carrying the step name and the answer key or plan name.
  • signin_google, signin_email, signup_email, signout — someone signed in, signed up or signed out. These carry no properties of their own.
  • paywall_view, subscribe_click, checkout_start, checkout_blocked, billing_portal, access_gained — the subscribe screen was shown, a plan was chosen, a checkout was started or refused, the billing portal was opened, or access began working in this browser. access_gained carries how it arrived: stripe, restore or local.
  • support_send, support_idea — a message was sent from the support page. The event records that it happened, and carries nothing else. What you wrote is not in it. Where the message itself goes is section 08.
  • owner_unlock — the site was unlocked with the owner's own passphrase.

None of these carries your email address, your name, or anything you typed into a box. Your Firebase user id is on all of them once you are signed in, and that id is the thread back to the account record in section 07.

Turning it off

Press the button. It takes effect immediately in this browser, is remembered, and stops both analytics scripts from loading at all on your next visit — not merely from reporting. Turning it back on takes effect when you next load a page.

Every analytics call on the site is wrapped, so a browser that refuses to load either script behaves identically to one that loads both.

06 · Payment, and the restore link

Subscriptions are taken through Stripe, on a checkout page that Stripe hosts. You leave the site to pay and come back when you are done. Your card number, your billing address and your name are typed into Stripe's page, not ours. They never touch our pages and we never see them. Stripe is responsible for what you enter there, under its own privacy policy.

Two things are added to that checkout address when you go to it. The first is your email address as Stripe's prefilled_email, so the payment page's email field is already filled in and you do not type it twice; you can change it there. The second is your Firebase user id as Stripe's client_reference_id — or, if you are not signed in, the random local id from fb_acct_v1. That is the entire link between a payment and an account: it comes back on Stripe's webhook and is what lets your subscription be written under your record. Nothing else about you goes with them.

When Stripe reports a successful payment, our webhook writes the subscription under your account. What it stores is in section 07.

The checkout's return address carries a success flag and a checkout session id. That session id becomes your restore token, stored as fb_pass_v1, and your restore link is that token on the end of a factbox.app address. It moves access from the browser you paid in to another phone. Signing in does the same thing and is the route we would rather you used; the restore link remains for anyone who subscribed before accounts existed.

The restore link is a bearer token. Nothing checks it against a server, so possession is the entire proof. Anyone you forward it to gets access, and so does anyone who reads it over your shoulder or finds it in a screenshot. Treat it like a key, not like a receipt.

It contains no personal information: it is a random-looking string plus a four-character check that tells a mistyped link apart from a real one.

07 · Your account, and what is held under it

An account is a document in Google Firestore, keyed by your Firebase user id. Four things write to it, and each writes somewhere different.

  • Firebase Auth holds the account itself: your email address, whether it is verified, your display name and photo if you came in through Google, when it was created and when you last signed in. Firebase holds your password; we do not.
  • Our Stripe webhook writes whether you are a subscriber, your Stripe customer id, and, for each subscription, its id, status, price, amount, currency, billing interval, whether it is set to cancel, when the period ends and when any trial ends. Nothing about a card is in it. A browser cannot write here — the rules forbid it — which is why it is worth trusting.
  • The site itself copies part of fb_acct_v1 to customers/{your id}/profile/onboarding. The whole of what that document may contain is fixed in our database rules, and it is this and nothing else: the subjects you picked, your minutes a day, your streak, what draws you to history, which statements sounded familiar, the plan questions' three answers, how often you said you read, the plan you picked, your first name, your email address, the random local id from §02, and whether the questions were finished. Nothing is inferred, scored or bucketed; a question you skipped produces no field at all, and an answer you change is changed there too.
  • The site also writes your reading and your saved stories to customers/{your id}/profile/reading. That is section 08.

Four of the six questions at the start of signing up — why you are here, what usually stops you, how much of a day goes to scrolling, and who you want to be a year from now — are not in that record and are not sent anywhere. They are stored in this browser only, under fb_acct_v1, because the rules that fix the shape of the document above do not name them. Choosing "let Factbox decide" rather than a number of minutes also stores nothing there. What does leave, for these four, is the measurement in section 05, which records which answer was tapped without being tied to that record.

Story text for subscribers is served by a function of ours that verifies your sign-in token and reads the subscriber flag itself, rather than believing anything the browser says. Its ordinary server logs are Google Cloud's, under Google's terms.

08 · Your reading, and where it now goes

This section changed. An earlier version of this policy said your reading never left your browser. That was true of an earlier version of the site and it is no longer true, because reading now follows an account rather than a device. What follows is what the code does today.

Your reading progress is: which stories you opened, how far into each you got, how long ago you last touched each one, which you finished, and which you saved. Signed out, all of it is written to your own browser and to nothing else — we do not have it and no page has an endpoint to send it to.

Signed in, the same information is also written to customers/{your id}/profile/reading in Firestore, under your Firebase user id. It holds a story id against four numbers — the furthest card, how many cards that story had, a whole-second timestamp, and a finished flag — plus the list of stories you saved, and counts of both. It holds nothing else; the shape is fixed in our database rules and capped in size there. This is what carries your place in a story to a second phone, and it is the reason signing in on a friend's phone does not show them your reading: the browser's copy is tagged with the account it came from and is thrown away when a different account signs in.

The trade has moved and is worth stating plainly. Your reading is now restorable — sign in anywhere and it comes back — and in exchange it is now something we hold, and so something that could be demanded of us or leak from us. Clearing your site data no longer deletes it; section 11 says how to have it deleted. The one thing that leaves in both cases is the measurement in section 05, which says a story was opened and for how long, not a list of what you have read.

What you write on the support page, and in the cancellation note

What you type into the two boxes on the support page does leave your browser. Pressing Send posts what you wrote to a function of ours, which writes one document into a support collection that we read in the Firebase console. The document holds six things and no others, and the function builds every one of them itself rather than accepting them from the page: the time, the reply address you typed if you typed one, which of the two boxes it came from, your message, the page you sent it from, and your Firebase user id if you were signed in. Your IP address is not stored, and neither is a hash of it. Messages are capped at 4,000 characters.

If that post does not go through, the page offers you a link that opens the message in your own mail app instead, addressed to hello@factbox.app, with what you wrote already in it. Nothing is sent that way until you send it yourself. If you are signed in, the reply box is filled in with the address on your account; you can change it or empty it.

09 · Hosting, and every other company involved

The pages are static files hosted on GitHub Pages. Like every web server on earth, it receives the ordinary request information needed to send you a page: your IP address, the address you asked for, your browser's user agent, and the time. That handling is GitHub's, under its own privacy policy. We do not receive or keep those logs.

Loading a page also makes your browser reach these, each of which necessarily sees your IP address:

  • Google Fonts — fonts.googleapis.com and fonts.gstatic.com, on every page, signed in or not, for the typeface the site is set in.
  • Google at www.gstatic.com, for the Firebase code that runs sign-in and analytics.
  • Cloudflare, for the analytics path described in section 05.
  • PostHog, when the Cloudflare path does not answer and the site falls back to reaching it directly.
  • Google Firebase — authentication and Firestore — once you sign in.
  • Google Cloud Functions at us-central1-factbox-7cb97.cloudfunctions.net, when you send a message from the support page and when a subscriber loads paid story text.
  • Stripe, when you go to a checkout page or the billing portal.

Every image is served from this domain. Artwork is downloaded from Wikimedia Commons once, in advance, and re-hosted here, so reading a story sends no request to Wikimedia and tells them nothing about you. Following a source or licence link from the credits page is an ordinary outbound link, and that site then sees you the way any site you visit does.

10 · Children

Factbox is not aimed at children under 13, and we do not knowingly collect anything from them or knowingly let them hold an account. If you believe a child has signed up, write to us and we will delete the account, anything sent from the support page, and everything else held under it.

11 · Your rights, and how to use them

Depending on where you live — the UK and EU under the GDPR, California under the CCPA, and many places besides — you have rights to see, correct, export or delete the personal information a company holds about you. There are two halves to that here, and they work differently.

  • What is in your browser: it is under the keys listed in §02, and your browser's developer tools will show you every one of them in full. To delete them, clear this site's data. On an iPhone that is Settings → Safari → Advanced → Website Data; Chrome and Firefox have the equivalent. That removes every key in §02, the cookies in §03, and the analytics services' own storage, in one action.
  • Note that this deletes your access too if you do not have an account, because access is one of those keys. Save your restore link first, or sign in, and it comes back.
  • What is under your account: clearing your browser does not touch it. Your name, email address, onboarding answers and — since reading moved to the account — your reading progress and saved stories stay in Firestore, and your account stays in Firebase Auth, until they are deleted. There is no button on the site that does this yet. Write to hello@factbox.app and we will delete the account, both profile records — the answers and the reading — and the subscription records under it, and confirm that we have.
  • If you have written to us: a message sent from the support page is a document in Firestore; an email is in our inbox. Ask for either to be deleted and we will delete it and confirm that we have.
  • If you have paid: Stripe holds a billing record, and has to, for tax and accounting reasons. Deleting your account does not delete that. A request about it can come to us and we will pass it on, or you can go to Stripe directly.
  • Analytics: the off switch in §05 stops future events. To have events already sent about you removed from PostHog or Google Analytics, write to us; we can ask both to delete the records tied to a user id, and we will tell you what came back.

We do not sell personal information and never have.

12 · Security

The account side is Google's: Firebase holds the passwords and the sign-in tokens, and the rules on our database are written closed, so a signed-in reader can read their own record and no one else's, and cannot write the flag that decides whether they are a subscriber. Paid story text is not readable from a browser at all; it comes from a function that checks the flag for itself.

Two limits are worth being plain about, because the alternative is you finding out later. The restore link is a bearer token, as §06 says. And for a reader without an account, the access flag is a value in their own browser rather than a check against a server, so it is something the site remembers rather than something the site enforces. Free story text is served as a public data file.

Email is not encrypted end to end. Please do not put anything sensitive in a message to us.
